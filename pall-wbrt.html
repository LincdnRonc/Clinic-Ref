WBRT: who and when

WBRT is now used less often. SRS is preferred for 1–4 metastases, and increasingly for 5–20. Reserve WBRT for disease that SRS or surgery can't treat, in patients whose prognosis is long enough to benefit. Give HA-WBRT + memantine when life expectancy is ≥4 months. For poor-prognosis patients, steroids and supportive care alone are reasonable (QUARTZ). SRS doses and decisions are on Brain metastases.

Where WBRT still fits

  • Multiple metastases unsuitable for SRS or surgery (too many, too large a total volume, or miliary), in a patient with reasonable prognosis.
  • Leptomeningeal disease: for nodular or symptomatic intracranial disease. Hippocampal avoidance is not appropriate. Consider proton CSI for fit patients with controlled systemic disease.
  • Salvage: diffuse new metastases after SRS, when repeat SRS is no longer practical.
  • After resection: only if cavity SRS isn't possible. N107C showed WBRT causes more cognitive harm with no OS gain.
  • SCLC brain metastases: traditionally WBRT. NRG CC009 (ASTRO 2026) found SRS gave longer OS with no difference in neurocognitive failure, so SRS is now reasonable for SRS-suitable disease.
  • PCI (SCLC): 25/10. MAVERICK (WCLC 2026) supports MRI surveillance alone where regular MRI is available; discuss PCI rather than giving it routinely.
  • Haematological: consolidation for PCNSL (reduced-dose), and CNS leukaemia or lymphoma per protocol.
  • Not routine after SRS or surgery for 1–4 metastases. It improves intracranial control but not OS or functional independence (N0574, EORTC 22952).

Systemic therapy during WBRT

  • BRAF ± MEK inhibitors: ECOG 2016 consensus is to hold for ≥3 days before and after fractionated RT (≥1 day for SRS), mainly because of vemurafenib skin and visceral toxicity. Newer data suggest continuing dabrafenib + trametinib is often tolerated. Agree a plan with the medical oncologist.
  • KRAS G12C inhibitors: data with WBRT are limited. In a KRYSTAL-1 case series, adagrasib was held for a few days around SRS. A short hold during WBRT is a reasonable default.
  • Immunotherapy: usually continued.
  • Cytotoxics: avoid giving radiosensitising chemotherapy concurrently unless it is part of a protocol. RTOG 0614 excluded chemotherapy from 14 days before WBRT to 14 days after.

Dose and fractionation

Neither survival nor symptom control improves with doses above 30/10. Ultra-short courses (10/1, 12/2) are worse. Choose by prognosis. Lower dose per fraction means less late neurotoxicity, which matters only for patients who live long enough to get it.

HA-WBRT hippocampal contouring and RTOG 0933 limits: CNS contouring guide, section 7.

  • Re-irradiation: there's no standard. Low total doses at ≤2–4 Gy per fraction have been used (e.g. 20/10). Check departmental practice.

Technique

Conventional WBRT
  • Supine, thermoplastic mask, CT slices 2–3 mm.
  • Opposed laterals, 6 MV. Cover the whole brain and meninges: cribriform plate, temporal lobe tips in the middle cranial fossa, and posterior fossa. Inferior border at C1–C2, with flash over the vertex.
  • Spare the lenses: put a non-divergent anterior edge (half-beam block or gantry/couch angle) behind the lenses, and keep the cribriform plate covered.
  • Use field-in-field to reduce hot spots. Limit dose to the scalp and ears where you can.
Hippocampal avoidance (NRG CC001)
  • 30/10 IMRT/VMAT. Fuse a thin-slice T1 contrast MRI and contour the hippocampi on it. Avoidance region is the hippocampi + 5 mm.
  • Hippocampus D100% ≤9 Gy, Dmax ≤16 Gy. PTV D98% ≥25 Gy. Also respect the optic apparatus constraints.
  • Not suitable for: metastases within 5 mm of a hippocampus, leptomeningeal disease, or an urgent start when planning time isn't available.
  • Simultaneous boost to gross metastases is investigational. SRS boost (RTOG 9508) gave an OS benefit only for a single metastasis.

Adjunct treatments

Memantine is the only drug with phase III evidence for preventing cognitive decline after WBRT. Amantadine is a different drug with weak evidence here, and it should not be combined with memantine. Dexamethasone is for symptoms, not for routine prophylaxis.

Memantine (RTOG 0614)

  • Start within 3 days of starting RT. Continue for 24 weeks.
  • Titrate: week 1 5 mg mane; week 2 5 mg BD; week 3 10 mg mane + 5 mg nocte; week 4 onwards 10 mg BD.
  • Renal: if CrCl is 5–29, the target dose is 5 mg BD.
  • Effect: delays cognitive failure (HR 0.78). Benefit was seen in executive function, processing speed and delayed recognition. Toxicity was similar to placebo.
  • Who: expected survival ≥4 months. The benefit takes months to appear, so it adds little for short-prognosis patients on 20/5. It's standard with HA-WBRT.
  • Side effects: dizziness, headache, confusion, constipation. Caution with seizures and anything that alkalinises urine.
  • AU PBS-listed only for Alzheimer's disease, so this use needs a private script. Write enough to cover the titration.

Amantadine Off-label, low evidence

  • What it is: a weak NMDA antagonist with dopaminergic effects. Used for Parkinson's disease, and off-label for arousal and fatigue after traumatic brain injury (Giacino, NEJM 2012).
  • Evidence in brain RT: no randomised trial in WBRT. A small open-label study of extended-release amantadine for radiation encephalopathy is underway. It is not a substitute for memantine.
  • Possible role: late fatigue, apathy or reduced arousal after brain RT, in specialist hands (neuro-oncology or rehabilitation).
  • Typical dose: 100 mg mane, increasing to 100 mg mane and midday. Avoid evening doses because of insomnia. Reduce the dose in renal impairment.
  • Do not combine with memantine. Both are NMDA antagonists and CNS effects can add up. Product information advises avoiding amantadine, ketamine and dextromethorphan with memantine.
  • Cautions: confusion and hallucinations (especially in older patients), seizures (monitor in epilepsy), livedo reticularis, oedema, anticholinergic effects.

Steroids and supportive care

  • Dexamethasone: only if symptomatic. 4–8 mg/day for mild–moderate symptoms. For severe symptoms, a 10 mg load then 4–6 mg q6h. Taper after RT as symptoms allow. Monitor glucose. Use a PPI if there are GI risk factors. Give PJP prophylaxis if steroids continue for several weeks.
  • Asymptomatic patients: no routine steroids. No antiepileptic prophylaxis (use levetiracetam only after a seizure).
  • Nausea: ondansetron. Consider it upfront for posterior fossa disease or raised ICP.
  • Scalp: wash gently with a mild shampoo, avoid heat styling, and use a hat or sunscreen. Moisturise dry, itchy skin.
  • Ears: warn about ear fullness or dulled hearing (serous otitis media). Check for otitis externa.
  • Hair: wig prescription or Look Good Feel Better referral before hair falls out.
  • Driving: follow Austroads seizure and neurological rules, and document the advice.

Other drugs for cognition and fatigue

  • Donepezil (Rapp 2015, n=198, ≥6 months after brain RT): no gain in the composite score. Memory and motor speed improved modestly, mostly in patients who were more impaired at baseline. Consider for late cognitive deficits.
  • d-Methylphenidate during RT (Butler 2007): no improvement in fatigue or QoL.
  • Modafinil/armodafinil: negative in RCTs overall. Possible benefit in patients with severe baseline fatigue.
  • Cognitive rehabilitation (e.g. Goal Management Training) is low risk. Refer for persistent deficits.
  • For radionecrosis, consider bevacizumab when steroids fail or can't be tapered.

Toxicity and timing

Brain toxicity comes in three phases. Acute effects occur during RT and in the weeks after. Early-delayed effects appear 1–6 months after RT and are usually reversible. Late effects start after 6 months and are progressive and often permanent. Higher dose per fraction, older age, vascular risk factors and concurrent chemotherapy all increase late risk.

Notes by phase

Acute
  • In QUARTZ, drowsiness, hair loss, nausea and dry or itchy scalp were all more frequent with WBRT. Serious adverse events were not.
  • For new headache, vomiting or deficits during RT, think of oedema: increase dexamethasone and image if it doesn't settle.
Early-delayed
  • Somnolence is more common and more marked in children. Reassure patients and families, and use steroids if needed.
  • Memory decline at ~4 months was 52% with SRS + WBRT vs 24% with SRS alone (Chang). Some recovery is possible.
Late
  • Severe dementia developed in 11% of 1-year survivors who had 3–6 Gy fractions (DeAngelis 1989). Onset was 5–36 months (median 14). It wasn't seen with ≤3 Gy per fraction. This is why long-survivors get ≤3 Gy per fraction.
  • For hearing, cochlear dose equals the prescription in conventional WBRT. The risk adds to cisplatin ototoxicity.
  • Hypopituitarism and second tumours matter mainly for long survivors (PCI, PCNSL, young patients). Check endocrine function if symptoms suggest it.

Key trials

The trials behind dose choice, neuroprotection, omitting WBRT, and the shift to SRS for 5–20 metastases and SCLC.
