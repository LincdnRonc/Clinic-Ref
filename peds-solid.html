Wilms tumour (COG)

Most children are cured with surgery and chemotherapy. Flank RT is for stage III and unfavourable histology. Doses are low.

When and how much

  • No flank RT: stage I–II favourable histology, stage I CCSK. Very low risk (<2 y, stage I FH, <550 g): no adjuvant therapy.
  • Flank RT (stage III, or any stage with unfavourable histology except stage I CCSK): 10.8/6; 19.8/11 for diffuse anaplasia or rhabdoid tumour. Boost residual disease.
  • Flank volume: pre-op GTV + 1 cm; cover the whole vertebral body to avoid scoliosis.
  • Whole abdomen (diffuse spill, peritoneal disease): 10.5/7; just over 20 Gy for diffuse unresectable peritoneal metastases. Limit the contralateral kidney to 14.4 Gy.
  • Whole lung: 12/8 if >12 months old (otherwise 10.5/7).
  • Stage IV/V FH with lung metastases: 4 y OS >90%.

Neuroblastoma and retinoblastoma

High-risk neuroblastoma: RT to the primary after surgery and stem cell transplant. Unlike RMS, treat the post-chemo/pre-surgery tumour. Retinoblastoma is now mostly treated without EBRT.

High-risk neuroblastoma

  • Dose: 21.6/12 to the tumour at surgery (ANBL 0532). Boost gross residual disease (previously 36 Gy to residual >1 cm³).
  • If the CTV reaches a vertebral body, include the whole vertebral body.
  • ANBL 1531 constraints: ipsilateral kidney mean 18 Gy; contralateral kidney 18 Gy to 25% and 8 Gy to 50%; liver mean 15 Gy; lung 20 Gy to 30%.

Retinoblastoma

  • Heritable (germline RB1) disease carries a high risk of second tumours, especially after RT. Avoid EBRT where possible.
  • Regional extraocular disease (stage II–III): chemo × 4 → 45 Gy involved-field RT.
  • EBRT to the globe if needed: 36–45 Gy.
  • CNS spread: 36 Gy CSI, 45 Gy cranium, 50.4 Gy pineal.

Osteosarcoma

A surgical and chemotherapy disease: RT is reserved for unresectable tumours, positive margins and palliation, and needs high doses.

Management

  • Peak incidence in adolescence (metaphysis of the distal femur, proximal tibia and proximal humerus); a second peak in older adults, often after Paget's disease or previous RT.
  • Work-up: MRI of the whole bone (skip lesions), CT chest (lung is the commonest metastatic site), bone scan or PET; biopsy at a sarcoma centre along the planned resection tract.
  • Standard: neoadjuvant MAP chemotherapy (methotrexate, doxorubicin, cisplatin), wide resection (limb salvage in most), adjuvant chemotherapy. Necrosis ≥90% after chemo predicts better survival.
  • Resectable lung metastases are resected; long-term survival is possible.

Where RT is used Verify

  • Unresectable primary (pelvis, spine, skull base) or patient declines surgery: 60–70 Gy or more; protons or carbon ions help reach these doses near the cord and bowel.
  • Positive or close margins where re-resection is not possible: 55–68 Gy to the tumour bed.
  • Craniofacial and older adults: more often irradiated because margins are harder to achieve.
  • Palliation of bone and lung metastases; SBRT for oligometastases is used, with limited data. Samarium-153 has been used for multiple bone metastases.
  • Osteosarcoma is relatively radioresistant: conventional palliative doses give shorter control than in other cancers.

Comparison with Ewing sarcoma

  • Ewing sarcoma is radiosensitive: definitive RT is an alternative to surgery for unresectable sites, and whole-lung RT treats lung metastases (see the Ewing tab).
  • Osteosarcoma has no routine role for whole-lung RT.

Written for this site; further reading: Tang and Farooqi, Pocket Radiation Oncology, 2nd ed. (2025), Osteosarcoma/Retinoblastoma/Brainstem Glioma chapter.

  • Neuroblastoma (malignant, least differentiated)
  • Ganglioneuroblastoma (intermediate)
