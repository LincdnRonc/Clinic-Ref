Know your two sequences

Check which breathing phase and which contrast you are looking at before drawing anything. T2 nav is a sharp end-exhale snapshot where fluid is bright; 3D Vane is a blurrier, motion-averaged T1 where fluid is dark.

Practical habits

  • Match the image to the delivery. T2 nav shows only the exhale position. For free-breathing ITV delivery the 3D Vane (average) position is closer to the time-weighted reality. On Unity CMM, expiration gating is tied to T2 nav and average gating to bTFE or T1 3D Vane.
  • Use both when available. Find the lesion and bile ducts on T2 nav; check edges and bowel gas on 3D Vane. If they disagree about organ position, that is breathing, not anatomy.
  • Scroll all three planes. Both are 3D acquisitions, so reformats are good. Duodenum, CBD and IVC are far easier to follow coronally.
  • Window aggressively. Narrow the window and centre on liver parenchyma; a mildly T2-bright met can vanish on a wide window.
  • Grey levels are relative. No Hounsfield units: judge each structure against its neighbours (liver vs spleen, lesion vs liver, fluid vs CSF).

What shade is each structure?

On T2 nav: water white, fat white-grey, solid organs grey, muscle dark grey; flowing blood, air, cortical bone and metal black. On 3D Vane (T1): water flips to dark, fat stays bright, liver and pancreas are the brightest solid organs.

Bile duct or vessel?

  • On T2 nav bile ducts are white and portal veins are black, running side by side in the portal triads.
  • Bright tubes heading to the porta and gallbladder = biliary. On 3D Vane the pattern reverses: ducts dark, vessels often bright.
  • Gas in non-dependent ducts after a stent or sphincterotomy (pneumobilia) is black on both, as are metal stents.

Common pitfalls and artefacts

Most daily contouring errors on the MR-linac come from mistaking breathing position or artefact for anatomy, or one tube for another.
