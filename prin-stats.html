Reading a trial

Error, power and p-values

TermMeaningUsual setting
Type I error (α)Concluding there is a difference when there is none (false positive)0.05 two-sided
Type II error (β)Missing a real difference (false negative)0.1–0.2
Power (1 − β)Chance of detecting the effect the trial was designed for80–90%
p-valueProbability of data at least this extreme if there were truly no differenceNot the probability the treatment works
95% CIRange of effect sizes compatible with the dataLook at the whole range, not only whether it crosses 1
  • Sample size rises with smaller expected differences, lower event rates, higher power and lower α.
  • Multiple comparisons inflate type I error: treat unplanned subgroups and secondary endpoints as hypothesis-generating.
  • A negative underpowered trial is "no evidence of a difference", not "evidence of no difference".

Effect sizes

  • Hazard ratio: relative instantaneous event rate over follow-up (Cox model); assumes proportional hazards. Check that the Kaplan–Meier curves do not cross.
  • Absolute risk reduction (ARR) = control rate − treatment rate. NNT = 1 / ARR. Quote absolute differences to patients.
  • Relative risk = treatment rate / control rate; odds ratio approximates RR only when events are uncommon.
  • Restricted mean survival time is useful when hazards are not proportional (immunotherapy curves).
  • Median follow-up and the number at risk under the curves tell you how much the tail can be trusted.

Survival analysis

  • Kaplan–Meier: survival estimate with censoring; compare curves with the log-rank test.
  • Cox regression: adjusts for covariates; gives hazard ratios.
  • Competing risks: when another event (e.g. non-cancer death in older men) prevents the event of interest, 1 − KM overestimates incidence. Use cumulative incidence (Fine–Gray) for local recurrence or cancer-specific death.
  • Endpoints: OS is unambiguous; PFS, DFS, MFS and local control depend on imaging schedules and definitions. Metastasis-free survival is a validated surrogate for OS in localised prostate cancer (ICECaP).

Phases

PhaseQuestionTypical design

IIIDoes it change practice?Randomised against standard; clinical primary endpoint

Superiority, non-inferiority and equivalence

  • Non-inferiority: shows the new treatment is not worse than standard by more than a pre-set margin (e.g. 5-year local recurrence within 2.5% or HR <1.3). Common in hypofractionation and de-escalation trials (START, FAST-Forward, CHHiP, PACE-B).
  • Judge the margin: is a loss of that size acceptable to you and your patient?
  • Per-protocol analysis is more conservative for non-inferiority; intention-to-treat dilutes differences and can make inferior treatments look non-inferior. Look for both.
  • Superiority trials use intention-to-treat as the primary analysis.

Bias and confounding

  • Selection bias: retrospective series of surgery versus RT compare fitter with less fit patients. Randomisation and allocation concealment remove it.
  • Lead-time and length-time bias: screening and better imaging (stage migration; the Will Rogers phenomenon with PSMA PET) make outcomes look better without changing biology.
  • Immortal time bias: patients must survive long enough to receive the treatment being credited (e.g. consolidative RT after chemo).
  • Performance and detection bias: unblinded assessment of subjective outcomes (toxicity, cosmesis).
  • Stratification balances key prognostic factors between arms; it does not replace randomisation.

Diagnostic accuracy

  • Sensitivity = TP/(TP+FN): rules out when negative (SnNout). Specificity = TN/(TN+FP): rules in when positive (SpPin).
  • PPV and NPV depend on prevalence: the same PSMA PET has a lower PPV for a solitary bone lesion in low-risk disease.
  • Likelihood ratio LR+ = sens/(1 − spec); multiplies pre-test odds to give post-test odds.
  • ROC curve area (AUC): 0.5 = chance, 1.0 = perfect discrimination. Calibration (does predicted risk match observed?) matters as much for nomograms.
