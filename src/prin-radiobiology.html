Core concepts

Fractionation works because late-responding normal tissues are more sensitive to dose per fraction than most tumours and acute tissues. EQD2 and BED arithmetic: EQD2 and BED calculator.

Linear-quadratic model

  • Cell survival S = e−(αD + βD²). α = single-hit (non-repairable) kill; β = two-hit (repairable) kill.
  • α/β = dose at which the two components contribute equally. High α/β (≈10): acute tissues and most tumours, insensitive to fraction size. Low α/β (≈1.5–4): late-responding tissues, prostate, breast, melanoma and some sarcomas, sensitive to fraction size.
  • BED = nd(1 + d/(α/β)). EQD2 = nd(d + α/β)/(2 + α/β).
  • Hypofractionation only gains a therapeutic ratio when tumour α/β is at or below that of the dose-limiting normal tissue (prostate, breast); otherwise the benefit comes from geometry (SABR).
  • The LQ model is less reliable above about 8–10 Gy per fraction; treat SABR BED figures as a comparison tool, not a biological truth.

LET, RBE and oxygen

  • LET (keV/μm): photons and electrons are low LET (<1); clinical protons are low LET except at the distal end of the Bragg peak; neutrons, α-particles and carbon ions are high LET.
  • RBE rises with LET to a maximum around 100 keV/μm (ionisation spacing matches the DNA double helix), then falls (overkill). Protons are planned with a fixed RBE of 1.1.
  • Oxygen fixes free-radical damage to DNA; it must be present at the time of irradiation. OER for low-LET radiation ≈2.5–3; it falls towards 1 with high LET.
  • Only a little oxygen is needed: radiosensitivity is half-maximal at about 3 mmHg.
  • About 1 Gy produces roughly 1000 single-strand breaks and 40 double-strand breaks per cell; the double-strand break is the lethal lesion.

Dose rate

  • Lowering the dose rate (LDR brachytherapy, TBI) allows repair during delivery and spares late tissues, much like fractionation.
  • Inverse dose-rate effect: in a narrow range, lower dose rate can kill more cells, because cells accumulate in G2/M.
  • Pulsed-dose-rate brachytherapy delivers hourly HDR pulses to mimic LDR.

DNA repair and cell death

  • Non-homologous end joining: fast, error-prone, active throughout the cycle (dominant in G1). Ku70/80 and DNA-PKcs.
  • Homologous recombination: accurate, needs a sister chromatid (S/G2). ATM, MRN, BRCA1/2, RAD51. HR-deficient tumours (BRCA) are more radiosensitive and PARP-inhibitor sensitive.
  • Mismatch repair loss (MLH1, MSH2, MSH6, PMS2) → MSI-high; predicts immunotherapy response (rectal dMMR: immunotherapy first).
  • Mitotic catastrophe is the main mode of death in most solid tumours after RT; apoptosis dominates in lymphocytes, haematopoietic cells and salivary serous cells; senescence contributes in slow tumours (PSA can keep falling for years after prostate RT).
  • Radiosensitivity syndromes: ataxia-telangiectasia (ATM) is the classic severe one; caution also with Nijmegen breakage, Fanconi anaemia, Li–Fraumeni (TP53, second cancers), and active connective tissue disease.

Consequential late effects follow severe acute damage that never heals (e.g. mucosal ulcer → fistula or stricture). Avoiding severe acute toxicity protects against them.

Serial and parallel organs

  • Serial (cord, brainstem, optic nerve, bowel, oesophagus): damage to a small segment loses the function of the whole. Limits are on maximum dose (D0.03cc, D1cc).
  • Parallel (lung, liver, kidney, parotid): function is lost only if enough volume is damaged. Limits are on mean dose or volume (V20, mean liver, critical volume spared).
  • Many organs are mixed (heart: coronary arteries behave serially, myocardium in parallel).

Skin reaction timeline

  • Faint erythema within days (vasodilatation); main erythema from about week 2–3.
  • Dry desquamation from about 30 Gy, moist desquamation from about 45–50 Gy in conventional fractions, peaking 1–2 weeks after RT ends.
  • Epilation after about 2–3 weeks; regrowth from about 2–3 months, often permanent above about 45–50 Gy to the follicles.
  • Late: atrophy, telangiectasia, fibrosis (dermis; dose per fraction dependent).
  • Skin sparing is lost under bolus, with tangential beams and at skin folds.

Hypoxia and dose modifiers

Tumour hypoxia

  • Oxygen diffuses about 150–200 μm from a capillary; beyond that cells become chronically hypoxic. Acute (perfusion-limited) hypoxia comes from transiently closed vessels.
  • Hypoxia predicts worse local control in H&N and cervix cancer and in sarcoma.
  • Anaemia matters clinically: keep haemoglobin up in cervix cancer (transfuse if low); avoid smoking during RT (carboxyhaemoglobin).
  • Nimorazole (hypoxic radiosensitiser) improved locoregional control in supraglottic and pharyngeal SCC in DAHANCA 5 (1998) and is standard in Denmark; not used routinely in Australia.
  • Hyperbaric oxygen, carbogen with nicotinamide (BCON in bladder), and hypoxic cytotoxins (tirapazamine) have mixed or negative results.

Radiosensitisers and protectors

  • Concurrent chemotherapy is the main clinical sensitiser (cisplatin, 5-FU/capecitabine, mitomycin, temozolomide, gemcitabine). Agents and doses: Concurrent chemo-RT.
  • Amifostine (thiol free-radical scavenger) can reduce xerostomia but causes hypotension and nausea; rarely used now that IMRT spares the parotids.
  • Targeted agents and immunotherapy with RT: timing and toxicity on Targeted and immunotherapy with RT.

Radiation and the immune system

  • RT can release tumour antigens and danger signals (immunogenic cell death) and upregulate MHC-I and PD-L1.
  • The abscopal effect (regression outside the field) is real but uncommon, even with checkpoint inhibitors.
  • Lymphocytes are very radiosensitive: large fields and many fractions cause lymphopenia, which is associated with worse outcomes in several cancers.

Radiation protection, pregnancy and fertility

Deterministic and stochastic effects

  • Deterministic (tissue reactions): threshold dose; severity rises with dose. Erythema, cataract, sterility, fetal malformation.
  • Stochastic: no threshold assumed; probability rises with dose, severity does not. Cancer and heritable effects.
  • Nominal risk of fatal cancer ≈5% per Sv for the whole population (ICRP 103). Heritable risk is much smaller (≈0.2% per Sv).
  • Second cancers after RT: latency about 5–10 years for solid tumours (shorter for leukaemia); sarcomas arise in or at the edge of high-dose regions, carcinomas (breast, lung, thyroid) in lower-dose regions. Young age at exposure is the strongest risk factor (Hodgkin survivors: breast cancer).

  • Below 0.1 Gy fetal dose, termination is not justified on radiation grounds (ICRP 84).
  • Check pregnancy status before simulation in anyone who could be pregnant. If treating during pregnancy, physics estimates fetal dose (mostly internal scatter, which shielding cannot stop); avoid high-energy (>10 MV) beams to limit neutrons.
  • Breast cancer in pregnancy: RT is usually deferred until after delivery.

Fertility Verify

  • Testis: ≈0.1–0.15 Gy causes temporary oligospermia; ≈2–3 Gy prolonged azoospermia; ≥6 Gy likely permanent. Sperm counts fall from about 6–10 weeks and recover over 1–3 years. Fractionated doses are more damaging than single doses. Offer sperm banking before pelvic RT.
  • Ovary: the human oocyte is very sensitive (LD50 <2 Gy). The dose that sterilises immediately falls with age: about 20 Gy at birth, about 16 Gy at 20 years, about 14 Gy at 30 years (Wallace 2005). Consider oophoropexy (transposition) before pelvic RT and fertility referral.
  • Leydig cells (testosterone) tolerate much more than germ cells (≈20 Gy in adults).
  • Uterine irradiation in childhood reduces uterine volume and raises miscarriage and preterm-birth risk.
