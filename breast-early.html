Breast (AJCC 8 anatomic stage)

DCIS

After BCS, whole-breast RT roughly halves ipsilateral recurrence, and about half of recurrences are invasive. It does not improve survival. Omitting RT is reasonable for small, low–intermediate grade, screen-detected DCIS with wide margins.

Decisions after BCS

  • Default: WBRT 40/15 or 42.5/16, hypofractionated (ASTRO 2018).
  • Boost: if ≤50 y, G3 or margins ≤2 mm (ASTRO). TROG 07.01 boost 16/8 improved 5 y freedom from local recurrence by 4.4% (93 → 97%), with slightly more grade 2 toxicity.
  • Consider omitting boost: >50 y, screen-detected, ≤2.5 cm, G1–2 and margins ≥3 mm.
  • Consider omitting RT: RTOG 9804-type DCIS (screen-detected, G1–2, <2.5 cm, margins ≥3 mm). RT still lowers recurrence (12 y IBTR 11 → 3%), so discuss the absolute numbers. The untreated risk is about 1% per year.
  • Endocrine therapy: tamoxifen lowers IBTR and contralateral cancer (B-24, UK/ANZ) but not mortality.
  • Margins: 2 mm is adequate for DCIS treated with WBRT (SSO/ASTRO/ASCO).

  • Invasive cancer: boost if ≤50 y, or 51–70 y with G3 or positive margin. Consider omitting if >70 y, HR+, G1–2 and margins ≥2 mm.

Partial-breast irradiation (APBI and PBI)

For low-risk disease, partial-breast RT gives local control comparable to whole-breast RT. 30/5 every other day (Florence) or 26/5 or 40/15 to the partial breast (IMPORT LOW) are the external-beam regimens to use. Avoid 38.5/10 twice daily.

Who is suitable (ESTRO-ACROP consensus)

  • Consensus criteria: age ≥50 y, luminal-like, ≤3 cm, G1–2, non-lobular, no LVSI, unifocal. G1–2 DCIS also qualifies if ≤2.5 cm with margins ≥3 mm.
  • No consensus was reached on margin (no ink on tumour), ITC-only nodes or prior systemic therapy.
  • In the GEC-ESTRO ASTRO consensus analysis, 5 y IBTR was 1.5% for suitable, 4.4% for cautionary and 8.8% for unsuitable patients.
  • Avoid 38.5/10 BID 3D-CRT (RAPID). It gave worse late cosmesis.

Omitting RT in older, low-risk patients

For older patients with small, ER+, node-negative tumours taking endocrine therapy, omitting RT increases local recurrence (about 1% per year) without changing survival. Endocrine therapy adherence is the key question.

Who to offer omission

  • ≥65–70 y, T1 (up to 3 cm in PRIME II), pN0, ER+, HER2−, G1–2, clear margins, willing to take 5 y of endocrine therapy.
  • LUMINA (Luminal A by Ki-67 ≤13.25%, ≥55 y) and EXPERT (PAM50 low risk) use biomarkers to identify patients who can omit RT.
  • If endocrine therapy is unlikely to be taken or tolerated, discuss RT (for example PBI or 26/5) as the alternative.

Key trials

CALGB 9343 (2004; 10 y 2013)636 · ≥70 y · stage I ER+ · tamoxifen ± RT10 y local recurrence 10 → 2%. No difference in OS, breast cancer survival or mastectomy-free survival.
PRIME II (2015; 10 y 2023)1,326 · ≥65 y · <3 cm · pN0 · HR+ · endocrine ± WBRT10 y IBTR 10 → 1% (5 y 4.1 → 1.3%). No OS difference. The authors say the results apply mainly to G1–2 ER+ tumours.

Commentary
Importantly, BCM is the endpoint used. The subanalysis of pathologic factors strengthens the findings. A trial large enough to show survival benefit in DCIS will likely not be performed. Do exercise caution using large population observational studies: A review found that the conclusions of these were not better than chance at matching randomized trials, and no factors were found to improve conclusions (Soni 2019). This does not necessarily mean the reports worthless - they still contain more data than a single insitution retrospective report for example - but that the eras and parameters should be carefully selected, that the results should be considered something in the realm of hypothesis generating and exploratory, and that results absolutely cannot replace or disprove a randomized trial unless perhaps it was poorly undertaken.

Commentary
73% of tumors were <1 cm. Note that if the recurrence rate is 1% per year, a patient will have a 15% chance of recurrence in 15 years and so on. There was no subanalysis of effectiveness hypofractionation vs. WBRT. The trial was in the 2D era and thus no dose constraints were included.

Note that RT is first choice per NCCN. It is more effective than TAM, is completed in a shorter time period, and has higher compliance, especially with hypofractionation. TAM causes cognitive decline (TAILORx, Wagner 2020). TAM adherence is unreliable: 20-60% will discontinue (Shinn 2019). In other trials such as UKCCCR, LC is superior with WBRT over TAM (Houghton 2003). On the other hand, it should be acknowledged that there is no OS advantage to either WBRT or TAM. Recurrences can be salvaged, but often with mastectomy. Regarding the lack of OS benefit, it should to be emphasized that many women with breast cancer "don't want to go through this again" and value the benefit of LC. One in ten women at 10 years is not a small number for all.

These trials emerged from an era when radiotherapy was 6 weeks. Noninferior regimens of 5 or 15 fractions have since been developed. A provocative essay entitled "Endocrine Treatment for 5 Years or Radiation for 5 Days" (Naoum 2022) makes a succinct point regarding the durations of the therapies.

The phase III trial that evaluated 10 Gy, Lyon, was single institution and found only a 1% benefit at 5 years. Some hold concerns for fibrosis with 16 Gy: the EORTC study shows 5% vs. 2% severe risk at 20 years, and 15% vs. 30% risk of moderate-severe. The EORTC study also benefits from longer-term f/u and subanalyses.

An intriguing question is, how should one view all the subanalyses? These are all post-hoc and not intentionally powered; in the era of this clinical trial, standards for subanalyses had not yet been developed. The subanalyses performed do meet a rational hypothesis however, so they seem reasonable to perform. Cancer cells don't know if an analysis was preplanned or not.

One could make a statistical argument that boost should be recommended in all lumpectomy patients based on the results of EORTC given that results in the entire cohort are positive, and that splitting the patients by subanalysis was not prespecified or intentionally powered. But, what matters is not if the analysis was planned, but whether it is rational and its degree of power - and most are not powered, but are still meaningful. Interestingly, in UK IMPORT HIGH, no such specific eligibility criteria were required, and that trial used 16 Gy boost as the control. For many rad oncs, boost recommendations are a balance between LR risk and fibrosis.

Likewise one should be cautious of fragmenting boost recommendations by decade as in the subanalysis: in addition to being exploratory, different patients of different life expectancies and comorbidities may have differing chances of benefit. Also recognize that division by decade is fully arbitrary: if boost in age 51-60 gives a significant benefit, there is more than likely also benefit in ages 51-62. Boost recommendations should be personalized just like RT vs. observation recommendations in DCIS and elderly invasive, or any medical decision.

TARGIT is a potential disruptor in oncology, and there have been questions related to the design of its arms, statistics, benefit, and mechanism of action. TARGIT is applied using a spherical applicator of 50 kV RT. This rapidly attenuates to 5 Gy at 1cm, leading some to question how it could provide adequate coverage (Polgár 2021). TARGIT authors have proposed biologic mechanisms related to avoiding seroma that may lead to increased effectiveness at lower depth (Belletti 2008).

Pre-path. 20% who had IORT crossed-over to receive WBRT for high risk features, which some say biases in favor of IORT. In the pre-path group, the authors did provide separate HR analyses for treatment received (IORT only, or IORT + WBRT) and found no difference in long term LRFS HRs. While some may question this methodology of allowing WBRT for high risk features, consider the alternative, where those with PBI-unsuitable risk features are withheld WBRT - an arguably unethical approach. Some remark that only the results of the delayed cohort matter - when surgical pathology is known - because this is the only way the arms can be truly balanced. A concern with delayed IORT is that there may be issues locating the original site, possibly different biologic mechanisms, and delayed IORT also isn't quite the point. The other alternative is to replicate the trial with more favorable eligibility upfront.

Statistics. Some had concerns that LRFS was reported in initial publication rather than LC. The authors eventually reported that LC is not different in the 2023 publication, p=0.28. Vaidya explains that LR does not censor those who have died, leading to misleading data (Vaidya IJROBP 2023). Some guidelines now do recommend that RFS type endpoints be standard (Vaidya BJC 2021, IJROBP 2023). A later meta-analysis by McMaster University evaluated IBTR and found that IORT and 2DCRT led to worse recurrence rates (Goldbergy 2022). Some have compared the results to PRIME II, asserting that IORT is no better than observation, but this is not sound: the populations are obviously different and such a comparison is not randomized or even propensity matched. TARGIT's population was higher risk and younger than PRIME II (Kunkler 2015, Vaidya 2021).

Some commented that the noninferiority margin or methodology was inappropriate (Cuzick 2015, Polgár 2021). The authors responded that appropriate statisitical methods were indeed used (Vaidya 2015). Later f/u was not planned, and rarely is, and thus noninferiority margins are not prespecified for late f/u.

Adverse effects. Can non-breast cancer deaths be explained by subtle adverse effects of EBRT? From Darby (2013) and van den Bogaard (2017) we know that cardiac events can occur within 5 years with RT. A later EBCTCG RNI 2023 meta-analysis shows how RT technique can impact later OS. Yet RT techniques have advanced, including with more sophisticated 3DCRT, VMAT, surface tracking, protons, etc.

Most of the statistical issues have been addressed, but some still have concerns regarding the mechanism and treating before final pathology. What if after final path you would have done something different? It is to be acknowledged that overall results seem favorable regardless, and costs are markedly reduced. In one editorial, Vaidya reiterates the comment by Zeitman (2016) that the "elephant in the room" of criticism of TARGIT is that revenue for many facilities will be impacted. In other words the study tested if SLN removal itself has any intrinsic benefit. The INSEMA authors write that omission of SLNB was must be weighed against potential benefit of RNI. A diagnostic method serves its role when it leads to a change in treatment that leads to benefit. It is also to be acknowledged that there is some good cause to avoid SLNB, most importantly an easier post-op recovery. The authors are applauded for their goal to eliminate potentially unnecessary procedures, and it is recognized that the trial was designed before the release of the EBCTCG RNI meta-analysis, when RNI was more controversial. Path

  • Graded based on nuclear pleomorphism, nuclear size, mitoses, necrosis, growth pattern

Growth patterns (C2PMS)

  • Comedo
  • Cribriform
  • Solid
  • Papillary
  • Micropapillary

Adverse features

  • Comedo pattern
  • Necrosis

Paget’s disease

  • Intraepidermal adenoCa with eczema of nipple due to epidermotropic spread.
  • Underlying DCIS more common than underlying invasive breast carcinoma

Phyllodes tumour

  • Biphasic epithelial and stromal tumour, leaf-like architecture.
  • Benign, borderline, malignant
  • Stromal features determine malignancy – cellularity, atypia, mitoses, stromal overgrowth, margins
  • Borders depend on nature
  • Malignant has preferential haematogenous spread over lymphatic
  • Axilla surgery not routine unless clinically suspicious nodes

LCIS

  • Classic: single discohesive invasive cell within lobules
  • Pleomorphic: pre-invasive cells have more atypical larger appearance with necrosis or calcifications
  • Florid: expansion of acini with pre-invasive cells, mass forming
  • Pleomorphic and florid should be completely excised with 2mm margin + adj RT
  • Increased risk for bilateral breast ca, 1-2% annual cancer risk

Screening

  • Screening MMG/US 2nd yearly from 50-74yo

Management

1. WLE +/- SLNBx if suspicious for foci of IDC

  • Obtain 2mm margins or re-excise

2. Whole breast adjuvant radiotherapy

  • Reduce LRR by 1/2 (EBCTCG MA, sweDCIS, MSKCC calculator)
  • 1% chance per year of recurrence up to 20yrs, half will be invasive (ECOG, EBCTCG MA)
  • 40Gy/15# (UK START B)
  • 26Gy/5# - 1% higher late tissue effect, apply more caution to large breasts due to increase separation and heterogeneity and age <50yo (FAST-FORWARD)
  • Boost for non-low risk DCIS: age<40 OR age>40 with: size >15mm, radial margin <10mm, IG or HG, comedo pattern, central necrosis, multifocal (TROG BIG)
  • DCISionRT genetic score – retrospectively validated in sweDCIS
  • Cardiac risk minimal (Danish cohort trials)

3. Partial breast radiotherapy

  • <20mm, unifocal, low-intermediate grade, margins >2mm, no BRCA, visible seroma, age >50
  • Conditional factors (should not have multiple): high grade, HER2 +ve in EBC, close margin <2mm, size 20-30mm
  • 40Gy/15# PBI or 40Gy/36Gy/15# reduced dose WBRT - IMPORT LOW
  • APBI 30Gy/5# - Florence trial
  • Multicatheter HDR brachytherapy (GEC-ESTRO ph 3 RCT)
  • Single channel HDR brachytherapy (Mammosite trial)
  • Brachytherapy on trial (ABPI – HEARTBEAT)
  • Intra-op 20Gy/1# - TARGIT

4. Endocrine therapy

  • not routine in AU however, tamoxifen shown to reduce IBTR, esp if not giving RT
  • Baby tamoxifen: 5mg for 3 years reduces recurrence risk by 50% at 10 yrs with lower toxicity than full dose tamoxifen (TAM-01)

Late Toxicity pathogenesis

Central pathway

  • stem cell depletion, fibroblast activation, vascular injury
  • Radiation → DNA damage + ROS → endothelial dysfunction → chronic inflammation (TGF-B) → fibroblast activation → fibrosis + microvascular loss → hypoxia → perpetuates fibrosis
  • Other cytokines: TNF-a, IL-1, IL-6, PDGF

Cardiac

  • Dose volume relationship – aim MHD <2Gy and LAD dose Dmean <10Gy, Dmax <15Gy
  • Endothelial injury → pro-thrombotic state → release TGF-B + microvascular damage and ischaemia → accelerated atherosclerosis → CAD
  • TGF-B → activation of fibroblasts to myofibroblasts → collagen deposition and ECM remodelling → constrictive pericarditis, constrictive cardiomyopathy, diastolic dysfunction, valvular disease, arrythmia
  • Depletion of myocytes further reduces regenerative ability
  • Synergy between RT and traditional CV RFs

Skin

  • Consequential late effects from basal keratinocyte depletion, failed repopulation, barrier dysfunction +/- infection → chronic ulcer Chronic inflammation → TGF-B → activation of fibroblasts → collagen deposition, fibrosis and induration
  • Fibrosis and endothelial cell damage → hypoxia → compensatory superficial telangiectasia
  • Melanocyte stimulation or apoptosis → pigmentation change
