Is it reirradiation, and should we do it?

Reirradiation evidence is mostly retrospective and consensus-based. Figures here are cited to their source; anything marked Verify was entered from memory. Constraints are starting points for an individual decision, not guarantees of safety. Have a colleague and physics review each case.

Decision statements worth knowing

  • Discuss alternatives and salvage options in a multidisciplinary team and with the patient; define and state the intent (S1, S3).
  • High-dose curative reirradiation is not recommended if estimated survival is under 6 months (S5).
  • Within 6 months of the prior course, weigh benefit against toxicity especially carefully (S7).
  • Do not offer high-dose curative reirradiation if the best response to the first course was progression (S8); judge on the response to and benefit from the first course, not on general radiobiology (S9).
  • Persistent grade ≥3 toxicity from the first course: discuss critically, taking the patient's risk acceptance into account (S6).
  • For limited life expectancy, reirradiation for symptoms may be considered even when cumulative doses are excessive (S2).
  • Prioritise target and OAR doses by life expectancy, risk acceptance and treatment goal (S17). Latency of irreversible toxicity can be shorter after reirradiation (S18).
  • RCR Principles of reirradiation (2024): discuss in the tumour-site MDT and in a separate radiotherapy forum; ideally at least six months from the first course; ECOG performance status 2 or better; classify by the ESTRO–EORTC system; consider biopsy; prospective peer review of contours and plans; cone-beam CT with online matching at every fraction; record cumulative EQD2 or BED with the α/β and any recovery used.

Agreement 76–100% across statements. Source: Andratschke 2022, Table 2.

Who does well: common threads across sites

  • Longer interval since the first course (head and neck RPA uses 2 years; most guidelines set a 6-month minimum).
  • Small volume, resected or resectable disease, good performance status, no persistent severe toxicity or organ dysfunction.
  • No or limited distant disease; a durable response to the first course.
  • A target that can be treated with a tight margin and no elective volume.

What to collect and how to add the doses

A working checklist built on the ESTRO–EORTC reporting items and the ReCOG 2026 dose-evaluation consensus.

At the first visit: collect

  • Every prior course: dates, site, dose and fractionation, technique, and the plan in DICOM (CT, structures, dose) if at all possible. Request it from the treating centre on the day.
  • Response and toxicity: best response, time to recurrence, acute and late toxicity graded by CTCAE, current organ function (swallow, lung function, bowel, bladder, neurology).
  • The recurrence: histology where feasible, restaging (PET-CT, MRI of the region), in-field versus marginal versus out-of-field.
  • The patient: performance status, comorbidity, life expectancy, systemic options, what risk they will accept.
  • Alternatives: surgery, systemic therapy, ablation, best supportive care: documented MDT discussion.

Reporting items: Andratschke 2022, Table 1.

Adding the doses

  • Prior plan available electronically: a 3D overlay of the dose distributions is mandatory (S15). Rigid registration first; deformable when anatomy has changed; always check the fusion visually.
  • Only fields, films or a summary: reconstruct conservatively (S14).
  • Nothing available: assume the prescription dose was given homogeneously to the region or organ (S13).
  • Convert before summing: use EQD2 or BED whenever fraction sizes differ (S16), with the α/β used for first-course constraints (S10).
  • Serial organs: judge on maximum dose. Parallel organs: judge on irradiated volume (S11).
  • Three levels of rigour (ReCOG case guide): direct point-dose summation; point-dose summation within the overlap region; image-registration-based 3D summation of equieffective dose. Choose by overlap complexity and how close an OAR is to tolerance.
  • Two accepted ways to set the new plan's limits: first-course constraints with a discount for the prior dose, or cumulative constraints on the summed dose (ESTRO/EANO 2025; ESTRO thoracic 2026).

Sources: Andratschke 2022; Paradis et al. ReCOG consensus, Lancet Oncol 2026;27:e101–10; ReCOG case-based guide (PMID 41985779).

Document in the plan

  • Reirradiation type (1 or 2), intent and goal.
  • Interval, prior dose to each OAR of concern, α/β and any recovery assumed, with the source of the constraint.
  • Registration method and summation method (point or 3D; physical or EQD2).
  • Cumulative OAR doses achieved, and which objectives were prioritised or compromised.
  • That the patient was told of the specific severe risks.

Planning habits

  • No elective volumes; tight GTV-to-CTV margins; daily volumetric IGRT.
  • Cascade: full target dose if OARs allow; then compromise PTV coverage next to the OAR; then change the prescription (ESTRO/EANO 2025).
  • Consider hyperfractionation where late toxicity dominates (nasopharynx, rectum) and SBRT where the overlap is small.
  • Tools: EQD2/BED and cumulative dose calculators · OAR dose limit calculator (Tim Wang) · remaining-dose calculator.

EQD2, BED and recovery

The arithmetic, and how much recovery it is reasonable to assume.

Formulas

  • BED = D × (1 + d / (α/β)).
  • EQD2 = D × (d + α/β) / (2 + α/β).
  • Use the dose per fraction actually received by the organ, not the prescription fraction size, when you have it.
  • Typical α/β: 2 Gy for cord and nerves (4 Gy lumbar cord in Nieder), 3 Gy for most late-responding tissues, 10 Gy for tumour and acute effects.

Survey of clinical practice: α/β of 3 Gy was the most common value; nerve structures 1.5–3 Gy (Stroom et al. Radiother Oncol 2025;210:111030).

Why SBRT changes the sums

  • A small high-dose volume with a steep gradient can keep serial-organ maximum dose low even inside a prior field.
  • But EQD2 at 6–10 Gy per fraction with α/β 2–3 Gy is large: 30 Gy in 5 fractions is an EQD2 of 54 Gy at α/β 3 and 60 Gy at α/β 2.
  • The linear–quadratic model is less certain above about 8–10 Gy per fraction. Verify

Consent and follow-up

What to say and how often to see them.

Consent points

  • The aim (cure, durable local control, or symptom relief) and the realistic chance of it.
  • Side-effects are more likely and can be more severe than the first time, can appear sooner, and some are irreversible or fatal (bleeding, fistula, necrosis, myelopathy, depending on site).
  • The evidence is limited; dose limits are estimates.
  • Alternatives, including no radiotherapy.
  • Site-specific rates are on the site pages in this section.

Follow-up

  • After high-dose reirradiation: every 3–4 months in the first year, then yearly, unless the risk of irreversible toxicity is low (S22).
  • Review by a radiation oncologist with imaging and examination (S21).
  • Record toxicity by CTCAE and feed it back to physics: local outcome data is how constraints improve.
  • Glioblastoma: MRI every 3 months, clinical toxicity check at 6 weeks (ESTRO/EANO). Lung: clinical review at 4–6 weeks, CT every 3–4 months for 2 years (ESTRO 2026).
