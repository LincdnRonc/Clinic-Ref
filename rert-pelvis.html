Locally recurrent rectal cancer

ESTRO 2026 recommendations (endorsed by ASTRO) and supporting series.

ESTRO 2026: who and why

  • Discuss every patient in a specialist MDT with experience in rectal reirradiation; planning and delivery by a team with reirradiation radiobiology expertise.
  • Discuss benefits, risks and uncertainties with the patient, including the aims of each option and effect on quality of life.
  • Resectable or borderline: consider neoadjuvant (chemo)reirradiation to improve the R0 resection rate.
  • Not resectable or not operable: consider definitive (chemo)reirradiation for prolonged local control.
  • Symptoms with poor performance status (WHO ≥2) or prognosis under 6 months: standard palliative regimens.

SBRTConsider for 1–3 small, discrete lesions without invasion of GI tract or bladder, as an alternative to surgery, in experienced centres (conditional)
  • Concurrent oral or intravenous fluoropyrimidine with neoadjuvant or definitive reirradiation if the patient is fit for it.
  • IMRT/VMAT for all neoadjuvant or definitive courses; daily volumetric imaging for SBRT and where a specific organ is of concern.
  • Neoadjuvant planning: prioritise PTV coverage where an R1 resection is likely; organs that will be resected can be deprioritised.
  • Protons or carbon ions, and regional hyperthermia: conditional, in centres with experience.

Cumulative dose

  • No evidence-based organ constraints exist for rectal reirradiation.
  • Panel view: a cumulative EQD2 (α/β 3) below 90 Gy to the PTV can probably be delivered without formal organ constraint calculations with acceptable severe toxicity; typical courses of 30–40 Gy give a cumulative 80–90 Gy. Not a universal safe threshold: read with organ-specific dose–volume data, overlap volume and intent.
  • Doses above 50 Gy improved local control in two studies at the cost of more severe late toxicity.

Outcomes quoted

  • Guren 2014 review (375 patients): median survival 39–60 months when resected, 12–16 months when palliative; symptom relief in 82–100%.
  • Pooled symptomatic response 75% in 413 patients (Lee review); non-operative 1-, 2- and 3-year OS 63.5%, 34.2% and 23.8%.
  • Non-operated series (Tao): late grade 3–4 toxicity 16%; symptoms improved in 70% for a median 10 months.
  • SBRT, UK multicentre (Johnson; 68 patients, 81 lesions, 30 Gy/5): 2-year OS 77%, median OS 38.7 months.
  • DESTROY-1 phase 1 (pelvic SBRT reRT, 5 fractions): up to 45 Gy after >60 Gy prior or prior small-bowel irradiation, 50 Gy after <60 Gy.

Sources: Peulen HMU et al. ESTRO recommendations for reirradiation in locally recurrent rectal cancer. Radiother Oncol 2026;223:111647 (full text); Guren MG et al. Radiother Oncol 2014.

Pelvic SABR reirradiation: nodes and sidewall

UK SABR Consortium guidance v2 (2025), the international Delphi (Slevin 2021) and the Leeds data.

Eligibility (UK SABR Consortium / NHS England)

  • Recurrence in the pelvis of a histologically confirmed pelvic primary; prior pelvic RT with no enduring significant toxicity.
  • Not for curative surgery (ineligible, declined, or positive margins).
  • More than 6 months since the first course; WHO performance status ≤2; life expectancy over 6 months.
  • Excluded: previously irradiated pelvic bone or spinal metastases; concurrent systemic or targeted therapy.
  • Do not use outside a trial if tumour infiltrates the lumen of the GI tract or the bladder wall.
  • Avoid with pelvic fibrosis, residual grade 3 toxicity from the first course, or inflammatory bowel disease.
  • Caution after brachytherapy (dose reconstruction) and beyond the tested limit of 3 lesions, each under 6 cm.
  • Delphi 2021: no consensus on minimum interval, lesion number or size, or cumulative constraints.

How the UK guidance sets organ limits

  • Cumulative limit = the usual de novo 5-fraction SABR constraint converted to EQD2. Example: small bowel D0.1cc 35 Gy in 5 fractions = 70 Gy EQD2 (α/β 3).
  • Convert the first-course organ dose to EQD2, then discount it for recovery: 30% at 6–18 months, 50% beyond 18 months.
  • Remaining EQD2 = limit minus discounted prior dose; convert back to 5 fractions.
  • Worked example from the guidance: 45 Gy/25 to small bowel one year earlier = 43.2 Gy EQD2; less 30% = 30.2 Gy; remaining 39.8 Gy EQD2.
  • α/β: 3 Gy for bowel, rectum, bladder, femoral heads and skin; 2 Gy for lumbosacral plexus and cauda equina.
  • Use less recovery after extensive pelvic surgery, with diabetes or vasculopathy, in older or heavily pre-treated patients.
  • The guidance calls this pragmatic and not evidence-based. Check any spreadsheet by hand. The remaining-dose calculator does the same arithmetic.

Working rules drawn from these

  • The parenchyma tolerates a second course when the standard first-course liver constraints are met again (for example ≥700 cc of uninvolved liver below 15 Gy in 3 fractions, and the usual mean-dose limits) and liver function is preserved (Child-Pugh A to B7).
  • Liver volume recovers within about 2–6 months in a healthy liver; cirrhotic livers recover less. Verify
  • The real limits are adjacent stomach, duodenum and bowel that were in the first high-dose region, and central bile ducts, especially with stents.
  • Sum the two plans (deformable registration where the liver has changed shape) and report cumulative mean liver dose and spared volume.
  • Selection used in the UK series: up to 4 metastases, uninvolved liver ≥700 cc, Child-Pugh ≤B7.
  • First-course reference: pooled classic RILD 0% and non-classic RILD 8% after SBRT for HCC (ISRS meta-analysis).
  • SBRT after radioembolisation: planning feasibility studies only; no clinical safety data.
  • MR-guided adaptive delivery helps when bowel sits against the target: see the MR-linac liver pages and the liver SBRT guide.

Bladder: as target and as organ at risk

Very little evidence treats the bladder as the reirradiation target. No guideline.

Bladder as the target

  • Main series: high-dose palliative pelvic reirradiation for genitourinary recurrences or second cancers after full-dose pelvic RT (28 patients, 16 bladder). Median reirradiation dose 50 Gy (27.5–66 Gy; 2.5 Gy fractions when under 60 Gy); median cumulative dose 113.9 Gy (81.5–132.8). All were symptomatic, inoperable and out of other options. Read the paper for outcomes and toxicity before quoting them.
  • I found nothing specific on radical bladder treatment after previous prostate radiotherapy. Cystectomy is the usual answer where feasible. Verify
  • Haematuria in a previously irradiated bladder: short palliative schedules (8 Gy × 1, 21 Gy in 3) are first-course data (MRC BA09); repeat use is anecdotal. Exclude radiation cystitis as the cause before reirradiating: hyperbaric oxygen and urological measures treat that. Verify
  • Tumour infiltrating the bladder wall is an exclusion for SABR reirradiation outside a trial (UK SABR Consortium).

  • Record baseline urinary function, capacity and any haematuria; small contracted bladders and prior TURBT or cystitis raise the risk. Verify
  • Reproduce bladder filling between planning and treatment.

Selection and work-up

  • Biopsy-proven local recurrence, usually ≥2 years after primary RT, with PSMA PET and MRI excluding metastases. Verify for the interval.
  • ESTRO ACROP Delphi (salvage SBRT): consensus on no age limit, ECOG 0–1, satisfactory urinary flow, exclusion of metastatic disease, and target defined on MRI.
  • No consensus on concurrent ADT, how to account for the first-course dose, or whether Phoenix criteria apply after salvage.
  • Focal salvage suits unifocal disease with imaging–pathology concordance; whole-gland otherwise.

Schedules reported

  • HDR brachytherapy: 19 Gy in 1 fraction (focal), or 12–13.5 Gy × 2 (24–27 Gy). Dose-escalated focal HDR up to 30 Gy in 1–2 fractions was reported as safe in the F-SHARP phase 1/2 (ASTRO 2024).
  • SBRT: commonly 30–36 Gy in 5–6 fractions. Verify
  • LDR (RTOG 0526): late grade 3 GI/GU events 14%; 10-year biochemical PFS 46%.
  • Systematic review (2021): 2-year bRFS about 74% HDR, 71% LDR, 55% SBRT; late grade ≥3 GU 7.9% HDR, 13.6% LDR, 2.7% SBRT.
  • Cumulative rectum and bladder limits from one SBRT series: see limits.

Sources: Valle LF et al. Eur Urol 2021;80(3); Jereczek-Fossa BA et al. Cancer Treat Rev 2021;98:102206; Crook J et al. RTOG 0526, IJROBP 2019; Front Oncol 2021;11:681448.

Gynaecological recurrence after pelvic RT

Mostly brachytherapy; evidence is retrospective.

Options

  • Central recurrence: pelvic exenteration is the surgical standard, with high morbidity.
  • Image-guided interstitial brachytherapy is the best-suited reirradiation technique for central and paracentral recurrences: more conformal than EBRT or SBRT in dosimetric comparisons (Bockel 2021).
  • Reported 2–3 year local control about 50% with interstitial brachytherapy.
  • SBRT for nodal and sidewall relapse: local control about 80% at 1 year and 50% at 2 years.
  • HDR schedules of 4–6 Gy twice daily for 5–10 fractions (EQD210 about 40–50 Gy) are common.
  • Better outcomes with small recurrences, longer interval, complete response and higher target dose.

Risks and consent

  • Fistula, bowel obstruction, vaginal necrosis and ureteric stenosis are the feared late effects; quote unit data where available. Verify
  • Refer to a centre with interstitial experience.
  • Cumulative EQD2 limits for rectum, bladder, bowel and urethra have been proposed from published series (Faundez 2026) but are not standardised.

Sources: Bockel S et al. Cancers 2021;13:1226; ecancer 2026;20:2091; narrative reviews of gynaecological reirradiation.
